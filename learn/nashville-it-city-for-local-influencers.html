The Nashville moment

Nashville is becoming the it city for local influencers

The moment is real, it is documented, and the founding roster for it is forming now.

The question

When did Nashville become an influencer city?

The Tennessean sent a reporter to find out, and the answer she brought back in July 2026 was a migration. Creator after creator has relocated to Tennessee in recent years, the paper reported, naming the LaBrant Family, Alex Warren, Kouvr Annon, Tabitha Swatosh, Hayley Trejo, Brittany Xavier, Cecily Bauchmann and Dani Austin Ramirez among the arrivals. A Casino.org study the paper cited had already ranked Tennessee fourth among states for Instagram influencers back in 2023, behind only New York, California and Washington, D.C. The region's 16 percent population growth over the past decade keeps restocking the audience, too.

Ask the creators who moved and the reasons repeat. "There's so much creativity in Nashville, so that was probably the biggest draw for me," comedian Danae Hays told The Tennessean, pointing also to the economics: Tennessee is one of eight states with no state income tax, and Nashville's cost of living runs 3 percent below average, per the Nashville Area Chamber of Commerce figures the paper cited. "Within the last two years, it feels like a southern L.A.," Hays said, "It feels more like a friendship instead of a business transaction here in Nashville." Lifestyle creator Haley Larkin, who relocated from Georgia, went further: "It's not just Music City. It's becoming a whole entire creator economy." She told the paper national brands now seek out Nashville creators to reach audiences, in her words, "that maybe weren't loving the L.A. vibe." Even the giants are local now: AreYouKiddingTV, with 7.7 million TikTok followers, moved to Nashville in 2022, and co-creator Steven Lannum told The Tennessean, "People realized they don't really need to be in L.A."

The money followed

Why are agencies and ad money moving in?

Institutions do not relocate for vibes. Both Creative Artists Agency and United Talent Agency, two of Hollywood's major talent agencies, run offices in Nashville, The Tennessean reported. CAA launched a dedicated influencer and digital creator division, CAA Creators, moved its Nashville headquarters into Nashville Yards and built content-capture studios into the new workspace. Two of its agents, Andrew Graham and Chris Wittine, developed a Creator Economy 101 class at Belmont University, described by the paper as the first of its kind. When an agency builds studios and a syllabus in a city, it is not visiting. It is settling.

The scale explains the urgency. CAA values the creator economy at more than $250 billion, calls it the fastest growing area in media and entertainment, and projects it to pass $1.25 trillion by 2035, figures The Tennessean cited from the agency. The IAB's 2025 Creator Economy Ad Spend & Strategy Report put United States creator ad spend at $37 billion in 2025, up 26 percent year over year and growing about four times faster than the media industry overall, with nearly half of ad spenders, 48 percent, calling creators a "must buy." Money at that scale hunts for supply, and Nashville is where the supply is concentrating.

The scene itself

What does Nashville's own creator scene look like?

A scene becomes a scene when it starts organizing and honoring itself. The Mall at Green Hills hosted the third annual Nashville Creator Awards on May 19, 2026, covered by Nashville Lifestyles in its "Behind the Screens" feature that August: five categories, Local Entertainment, Fashion, Family, Lifestyle and Home, with working local creators taking the honors. In September 2026, the Nashville Creator Summit convened local creators and marketers; the Nashville marketing podcast Lipstick Economy marked the season with an episode titled "The Creator Economy is Here!", featuring Guild Creative Group founder Tiffany Hardin, who describes her work as "culture A&R" for brands. Blogger Xchange runs a year-round organized creator community, workshops and a multi-day XPO where brands meet creators in person. City Lifestyle profiles the food scene's tastemakers by name.

Look at the working layer underneath the events and the pattern sharpens. On Feedspot's published Top 30 Nashville food influencer list, 19 of the 30 accounts sit between roughly 700 and 15,500 followers (counts as listed by Feedspot, October 2026). The accounts Nashville businesses actually get discovered by are not celebrities passing through. They are neighbors with a few thousand followers, a neighborhood beat and a comments section full of people who live close enough to act on a recommendation tonight. That is the league this moment is being built in, and it is worth understanding before the next section, because it decides who the moment belongs to.

The stakes

What did Michelin change?

In 2025, the Michelin Guide arrived in Tennessee for the first time as part of its American South guide, and Nashville immediately took three stars: Bastion, Locust and The Catbird Seat. On October 21, 2026, the guide held its American South ceremony in Nashville itself, at The Pinnacle, and the city's three starred restaurants kept their stars while new names joined the guide as recommended rooms and Maiz de la Vida earned a Bib Gourmand. Bastion's chef and partner Josh Habiger told The Tennessean the 24-seat restaurant has been full or nearly full every night since its star arrived in 2025. Nashville Convention & Visitors Corp president Deana Ivey called hosting the ceremony "a significant moment for Nashville and an opportunity to showcase our city's culinary scene on a national and international stage."

City Cast Nashville framed its Michelin coverage as what the guide's arrival means "for locals, chefs, tourists, and yes, influencers," with Nashville Scene food writer Chris Chamberlain breaking it down. That "yes, influencers" is the tell. A star makes a headline for a week. What fills the room for the year after is discovery: locals deciding where to eat on a Thursday, regulars reminding each other a ten-year-old room is still great, newcomers finding the places the guide has not reached yet. Discovery at that tempo is creator work, and in Nashville it is local creator work, because the audience that can act on it lives here.

The turn

So who does the it city belong to?

Here is the distinction the headlines miss. The relocated stars brought national audiences with Nashville addresses. The discovery economy, the one that fills dining rooms, salons, studios and listening rooms every week, runs on accounts whose followers actually live in the metro. Nashville is a visitor town: follower counts here mix neighbors with bachelorette parties and festival crowds who flew home on Sunday. A business in East Nashville or 12 South cannot seat a tourist next Tuesday. It can seat your neighbors. That share of an audience, the part that lives in the Nashville metro, is called local reach, and it is the number that predicts walk-ins. SIGNL puts it at the top of every creator profile, ahead of follower count, and explains the math in what local reach is and why brands care more than follower count.

The engagement data backs the small account, too. HypeAuditor's 2026 State of Influencer Marketing report found nano-influencers on Instagram, accounts of 1,000 to 10,000 followers, post the highest engagement rate of any tier at 1.78 percent, against 0.33 percent for accounts over a million followers. Small and local is not a compromise position in this market. It is the highest-performing one, and Nashville's own published lists, heavy with accounts between 700 and 15,500 followers, show the working roster already exists.
