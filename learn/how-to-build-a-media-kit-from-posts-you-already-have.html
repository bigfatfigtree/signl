How to build a media kit from posts you already have

You do not need a designer, a logo wall, or a past brand deal to have a media kit. You need one page that answers the questions a business asks before it books: who are you, where does your audience live, what have you posted that worked, and what do you charge. Every one of those answers is already sitting in your feed. This is how to pull them together in about an hour.

By Jeff, Founder, Magpie · November 18, 2026 · Magpie Learn hub

The Short Answer

A creator media kit is one page with five blocks: who you are, where your audience lives, three best posts, your categories and formats, and your rate. Build it from posts you have already published. Lead with local reach, not follower count, and keep it to one page a business can read in a minute.

Key Takeaways
  • Your feed is the raw material. No past brand deal is required.
  • Local reach goes first. Follower count is a supporting line, not the headline.
  • Pick three posts by what they prove: saved, shared, and talked about by locals.
  • One page, current numbers, your rate on it. That is a kit a business can act on.
The starting point

You already have the material. It is your feed

Most creators delay a media kit because they think it documents a career they have not had yet: sponsor logos, campaign photos, testimonials. For a local creator, that version is backwards. A local business is not buying your history with other brands. It is buying proof that people near it follow you, trust your word, and act on your posts. Your regular feed proves all three, if you pick the right posts and label them clearly.

So skip the blank page problem. Open your own profile, sort your posts by what they earned, and treat the kit as an editing job, not a writing job. You are selecting and labeling work that exists. That is why this takes an hour, not a weekend.

The five blocks

What goes on the page, in order

/ 01

Who you are, in two lines

Your name, your handle, the place you post from, and what you post about, in plain words. Two lines is enough. A business deciding on a visit needs to place you fast: a person, in its market, with a clear beat. Save the life story for your about page.

/ 02

Where your audience lives

This is the headline number, and it is your local reach: the share of your audience that lives in your market. Put that percentage and the rough count of local followers where a reader sees them first. Follower count can sit underneath as a supporting line. A smaller account with a mostly local audience is the stronger buy for a local business, and your kit should say so before anyone has to do the math.

/ 03

Three best posts, labeled

Three posts, each with one line on what it did. Not your three favorites, and not just your three biggest view counts. Pick them by the job they prove, which the next section covers. Link each one, or screenshot it cleanly enough that the post and its numbers can be read on a phone.

/ 04

Categories and formats

The kinds of businesses you post about well, and the formats you deliver: a post, a reel, stories. Be specific enough that a business can picture its own visit. If you only post honestly about places you would spend your own money on, say that too. It is a selling point, not a limitation.

/ 05

Your rate and a way to reach you

End with your rate for a paid post and the best way to contact you. A kit without a rate makes the business guess, and guessing usually ends the conversation. If you are still setting your price, work out what a small creator should charge first, then put the number on the page. Clear beats clever here, every time.

Picking the proof

Choose three posts by what they prove, not by views

Views are the easiest number to find and the weakest proof for a local booking. A view can come from anywhere and mean very little. The signals a local business should care about are closer to a purchase: a save means someone plans to act, a share means your audience trusts your pick enough to pass it on, and a comment from a local means the post started a conversation with the exact people the business serves.

  • ✓One saved post: a recommendation, a menu, a before and after, anything people kept for later. Label it with its save count if you can see it.
  • ✓One shared post: the post your followers sent to friends. Shares are your distribution working without an ad budget behind it.
  • ✓One local conversation: a post where people nearby commented, tagged friends, or asked where it was. That thread is the audience, talking.

Under each post, write one honest line: what it was, and what it did. Keep the numbers you can back up, and skip any number you cannot. Businesses have seen inflated kits before, and one claim that does not survive a click costs more than a modest number stated plainly. This is also what brands check first when they read a profile, as what brands look for in local creators lays out.

The build

Assemble it in about an hour

Use whatever tool you already have: a single page in a free design app, a clean document, or one simple web page. The format matters far less than the order and the clarity. Work in this sequence:

  • ✓First 15 minutes: pull your numbers. Follower count, local reach share, and the rough local count, from your platform analytics.
  • ✓Next 20 minutes: pick and label the three posts, one saved, one shared, one with local comments, and write the one line under each.
  • ✓Next 15 minutes: write the two line intro, your categories and formats, and your rate.
  • ✓Last 10 minutes: read it on your phone, out loud, as if you owned a business nearby. Cut anything that does not help you decide in a minute.

Then save it where you pitch from: a link you can drop into a DM or an email, and a PDF copy for anyone who asks for a file. One page keeps both versions identical, which is the point. When you send it, the pitch around it stays short: who you are, one line on your local audience, and the kit attached. The same discipline that works in negotiating a deal without an agent works here: specific, written, and easy to say yes to.

Common mistakes

Five things that make a kit weaker

  • ✓Follower count as the headline: it invites a comparison you may lose to a bigger, less local account. Lead with local reach instead.
  • ✓Old numbers: a kit from six months ago understates a growing account and dates you on sight. Refresh the numbers monthly, and after every booking.
  • ✓Unreadable screenshots: tiny, cropped, or blurry post images read as careless. Link the live posts, or capture them at full size.
  • ✓No rate, no contact: a kit that ends with neither forces a second conversation before a first booking can even be discussed.
  • ✓Logos you cannot back up: brands you bought from, were gifted by, or simply like are not clients. List only work you were actually booked to do.

Straight Answers

What else do creators ask about media kits?

What should a creator media kit include?

Five things, on one page: who you are and what you post about, where your audience lives with your local reach as the headline number, three of your best posts with a line on what each one did, the categories and formats you take bookings in, and your rate with a way to reach you. That is the whole kit. Anything beyond those five is decoration, and decoration is where most media kits go wrong.

How do I make a media kit if I have never had a brand deal?

Use your regular posts. A media kit is proof of what you publish and who sees it, not a list of past sponsors. Pick the three posts that earned the most saves, shares, and comments from people who live near you, state your local reach and your categories, and list the rate you would charge for a first booking. A creator with no brand deals and a clear one page kit looks more bookable than a creator with logos and no numbers.

Which posts should I put in my media kit?

Pick by the job each post proves, not by views alone. Choose one post that got saved, because saves show people plan to act on it. Choose one that got shared, because shares show your audience passes your word along. Choose one that drew comments from locals, because local comments are the audience a business is actually buying. For each post, write one line: what it was, and what it did.

How long should a creator media kit be?

One page. A local business reads a media kit the way it reads a profile, in under a minute, usually on a phone. One page forces you to lead with local reach, your three best posts, and your rate, which are the three things that decide a booking. If you have more to say, link to your profile and your best posts and let the work carry the rest.

Do I need a media kit to get booked on Magpie?

No. On Magpie your profile is the kit: businesses see your local reach, your categories, your listed rate, and your delivery record before they book, and that record updates as you post. A media kit still helps for pitches you make off the platform, in a DM or an email, where there is no profile doing that work for you. Build the one page version once, keep it current, and use it wherever your profile is not in the room.
