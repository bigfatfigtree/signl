How to track micro-influencer marketing ROI

By Jeff, Founder, Magpie · October 22, 2026

You track micro-influencer ROI the way you track anything at a register: tie spend to specific posts, count only what you can prove as the floor, and label everything else as a model. Creator-specific codes give you tracked register spend per creator. Engagement can be converted into a media value, but that is an estimate and has to be labeled one. Here is the honest stack, layer by layer.

The short answer: Count what the register proves, and label everything else as modeled. When a business connects Square, Magpie reads back what each creator's code actually rang up at the register; that counted number is the floor and it is never blended with estimates. Engagement-based media value sits above it, clearly labeled as modeled.

Layer one

What can you actually count?

Tracked register spend. Each creator carries a unique code, their followers use it at your register, and you tally what those codes bring in. That number is tied to a specific creator and a specific post, and it is counted in dollars by your own till.

Treat it as a floor, because that is what it is. The tally only counts guests who used the code. It misses the follower who saw the post, came in a week later and paid full price without mentioning it, and it misses the regular the post created who returns all winter. Both of those are real value the code cannot see. A floor that undercounts is the honest starting point: everything you build on it is provable, and the true number is above it, not below.

This is also the layer that makes rebooking a rational decision instead of a vibe. When the tally says one creator's visits reliably out-pull another's at your register, you book more of the first and less of the second, and your cost per real customer falls every month you do it.

Layer two

What is media value, and why must it be labeled?

Media value converts a post's engagement, views, likes, comments and shares, into a dollar figure using a modeled rate, the way you would price equivalent advertising. It is genuinely useful for comparing posts against each other. It is not revenue, and any report that presents it as revenue is inflating.

The distinction sounds picky until real money follows it. A dashboard that blends tracked spend and modeled value into one big number can make a weak month look strong and a strong creator look average. Magpie keeps the two apart on purpose: the Magpie ROAS Score leads with tracked register value, and the media value beside it is labeled as modeled, every time. When you compare your program month to month, compare floor to floor and model to model, never a blend to either.

The sample, labeled as a sample

The cost side

What belongs on the cost side of the math?

All of it, at its real cost. A return figure is only honest if the denominator is.

/ 01

The plan

Your subscription for the month: $199 for Self-Service, $349 for Growth or $699 for Managed, the same published pricing as the plans page. No other platform fees hide inside standard visits.

/ 02

The comp, at cost

Count the comp at what it costs you to provide, not at menu price. A $100 dinner costs a restaurant its food and labor cost, not $100. Counting retail price on the cost side while counting real dollars on the return side quietly rigs the math against the program.

/ 03

Paid bookings, if any

Optional paid visits count at what you actually paid: the creator's listed rate plus 3 percent, with the creator keeping 97 percent of their rate. Most programs run mostly on comps, so for many businesses this line is zero, and it should say zero rather than disappear.

That discipline is why the sample above shows two numbers instead of one: a headline score on tracked value, and an all-in score with the complete cost side counted. Ask any platform, including this one, which of the two a quoted figure is. If they cannot answer, the number is marketing.

The routine

How do you run this every month?

Four habits, none of them technical. The tracking only works if the register end of it actually happens, and that part is a routine, not a tool.

Give every creator their code before the visit, and make sure the team on shift knows it exists. Report tallies on a rhythm, weekly is plenty, so codes get credited to the right creators while the month is still fixable. Compare creators on tracked spend per visit, not on follower counts or how the post felt. Rebook what the floor proves and rotate out what it does not, kindly and without drama: the roster is deep, and fit varies by business.

Run that routine through a concentrated week and it becomes the scoreboard for the week itself, which is exactly how the Black Friday plan is judged. Run it all year and you own something most local marketing never produces: a per-creator record of what a post actually did at your register, priced honestly, that gets sharper every month.

FAQ

Questions, answered straight

How do small businesses measure influencer marketing ROI?

In layers. First, tracked register spend: each creator carries a unique code, and the business tallies the spend those codes bring in, per creator and per post. Second, media value: engagement converted to dollars at a modeled rate, always labeled as modeled. The cost side counts the plan, the comp at actual cost, and any paid bookings. Return is read against that full cost.

What is a creator code, and how does it work?

It is a unique code tied to one creator, shared in and around their post, that their followers can use when they visit. When a guest uses it, the business tallies that spend to the creator. The code is how a post stops being a guess and becomes a number the business counted itself.

Is tracked register spend the same as revenue from the post?

It is register spend from guests who used the creator's code, counted by the business. Treat it as a floor, not a total: it undercounts, because guests who saw the post and paid without the code are invisible to it. It should never be presented as the total revenue a post generated.

What does the Magpie sample dashboard show?

Magpie's published sample dashboard shows a Magpie ROAS Score of 6.29x on tracked value and 5.06x all-in with the full cost side counted. The business on it, Harbor Table, is a sample business and every number is illustrative, published to show the format of the reporting. It is not a result any specific business earned.

What should I be suspicious of in an ROI report?

Three things: modeled media value presented as revenue, a single blended number that mixes tracked spend with estimates, and a cost side that counts comps at full retail price or leaves costs out entirely. An honest report separates what the register counted from what a model estimated, and shows its full cost side.
