FTC disclosure for comped posts

The rule

Why does a free meal need a disclosure at all?

Because the meal changes the relationship, and your audience is entitled to know it. The FTC's Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255, most recently revised in 2023) say that when someone endorsing a product has a material connection to the business, a relationship that might affect how much weight people give the endorsement, that connection has to be disclosed.

A material connection is not only cash. Free products, free services, free experiences, discounts and early access all count, which is why a comped dinner is squarely inside the rule. The logic is simple: a post that reads like a spontaneous discovery lands differently once a follower knows the visit was provided. Disclosure does not weaken a post; it is what makes an honest post believable, and it protects the creator and the business at the same time.

The source documents are public and readable. Start with the FTC's own plain-language explainer, FTC's Endorsement Guides: What People Are Asking, which walks through exactly these situations, including free products and social posts.

The placement

What makes a disclosure clear and conspicuous?

The FTC standard is that a disclosure must be difficult to miss and easy to understand. Three habits get you there on every format.

/ 01

Say it in words

Use plain words a follower cannot misread: "#ad", "hosted by" plus the business name, or "free meal from" plus the business name. Ambiguous tags like #collab, #ambassador or #blessed do not tell anyone the visit was provided, and the FTC does not treat them as clear.

/ 02

Put it where it is seen

At the start of the caption, on screen in the video, or spoken in it. Not at the end of a long hashtag pile, not in a comment, not only in a location tag, and not behind a "see more" tap. If a follower has to hunt for it, it is not conspicuous.

/ 03

Every format, every time

Posts, reels and stories each need their own disclosure; one on the feed post does not cover the stories around it. In stories, put the words on the frames themselves, big enough to read. And do not rely on a platform's paid partnership label alone: the FTC has said built-in tools can be easy to miss, so add your own clear words too.

The shared duty

Who is responsible: the creator or the business?

Both. The creator makes the post, so the creator makes the disclosure. The business provided the comp, so the business is responsible for asking for disclosure, never for asking anyone to hide it, and for paying attention to what gets posted.

That shared duty is why SIGNL treats disclosure as part of the deliverable, not a courtesy. On a comped creator visit, the Standard Visit is a post plus one story that shares it, honest and disclosed, to a 48-hour standard, and delivery is tracked with the disclosure included. A business that asks a creator to skip the disclosure is asking for a violation and is removed from the platform. A creator who hides a comp is risking their account with the FTC and their standing on the roster.

One more line that matters: disclosure is about the relationship, not the opinion. Disclosing a comp does not mean the post is an ad read, and it never licenses a business to script it. The post stays the creator's honest take, labeled with how the visit happened. Those are two separate rules, and both protect the same thing: a follower who can trust what they are reading.

The checklist

What should you check before a post goes live?

Thirty seconds, five checks, before anything posts.

One: the disclosure is in the first lines of the caption or on screen, in plain words, with the business named. Two: every story in the set carries it on the frame, not just the first one. Three: nothing in the post claims something you do not know is true, like a health benefit or a "best in the city" superlative you cannot back up. Four: the opinion is yours, start to finish. Five: if the business asked you to change any of the above, that is a red flag worth reporting, on SIGNL or anywhere else.

FAQ

Questions, answered straight

Do you have to disclose a free meal from a business?

Yes. A free meal, service or experience is a material connection under the FTC's Endorsement Guides, and material connections must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously. It does not matter that no cash changed hands; the free visit itself is the connection your audience is entitled to know about.

Is #ad enough of a disclosure?

#ad works when it is easy to see: at the start of the caption, on screen in a video, or spoken in it. Buried at the end of a long hashtag list, placed in a comment, or used on its own in a location tag, it is not clear and conspicuous. Words like hosted by, plus the business name, are clearer still.

Do stories need a disclosure too?

Yes. Stories are endorsements like any other format, and each story about a comped visit needs its own disclosure on the frame, large enough to read in the time the frame is up. A disclosure on your feed post does not cover your stories, and a disappearing format does not mean a disappearing rule.

Is the platform's paid partnership label enough by itself?

No. The FTC has said that built-in platform disclosure tools alone may not be enough, because they can be easy to miss or misunderstand. Use the label if it is there, and also add your own clear words in the caption, on screen, or out loud in the video.

Can a business ask a creator not to disclose a comp?

Never. Asking a creator to hide a material connection asks them to break the FTC's guides, and it exposes both the creator and the business. On SIGNL, disclosure is a required part of every visit's tracked delivery, and a business that pressures a creator over it is removed from the platform.
