What a fair exclusivity clause looks like for a local creator

An exclusivity clause is a promise not to work with certain other brands for a while. That promise can be fair, and it can be a trap. The difference is in three words most clauses leave vague: which category, which place, and how long. Name all three, price what the clause blocks, and you have a deal you can judge. Leave any one of them open, and one small booking can quietly lock up a season of work.

By Jeff, Founder, Magpie · November 14, 2026 · Magpie Learn hub

For creators How to negotiate without an agent
The plain definition

Exclusivity is not a compliment. It is a block on future work

When a business asks for exclusivity, it is not asking for your post. It already has that in the booking. It is asking for all the posts you will not make for anyone else while the clause runs. That is why the clause deserves its own read, separate from the deliverable, the deadline, and the price of the post itself.

For a local creator, that block matters more, not less. Your value is concentrated: you post about one market, in a handful of categories your neighbors actually follow you for. A broad clause does not trim a corner of your business. It can pause the exact category and place where most of your bookings live. This is general information about how these clauses work, not legal advice, and a contract that matters is worth a lawyer's read. But the fairness test below is one any creator can run in five minutes, before anything is signed.

The fairness test

Three limits make a clause fair

/ 01

Category, named narrowly

Fair: you will not post for another coffee shop during the window. Not fair: you will not post for any food, drink, hospitality, or lifestyle brand. The first names a real competitor set. The second swallows categories you earn in. If the category is wider than the business's actual competitors, narrow it, in writing.

/ 02

Place, named clearly

A local booking should carry a local limit. Fair: the same market where the business sits. Not fair: a ban that follows you everywhere, including places that business will never serve. Your local reach is market by market, so the limit should be written market by market too.

/ 03

Time, with a real end date

Fair: a short window around the post, with a calendar date where it ends. Not fair: no end date, or a long tail after a single post. Like usage rights in perpetuity, an open ended limit takes the most while sounding like a small line. If there is no end date on the page, there is no fair clause yet.

Red flags

Five lines that should stop the pen

  • ✓No end date: exclusivity that runs until the brand says otherwise, or that renews on its own.
  • ✓Everywhere and everything: a ban on a whole industry, a whole platform, or work in places the business does not serve.
  • ✓Unpaid: the clause sits inside a standard rate, or inside a comp, with no line item of its own.
  • ✓It starts before the post: a long lead in plus a long tail means one post blocks months on both sides of it.
  • ✓It covers your own life: wording so broad it reaches personal posts, other income, or categories you were posting about long before this brand arrived.

Any one of these is a reason to rewrite, not a reason to panic. Most bad clauses are not malice. They are a template written for a national campaign, pasted onto a local booking where it does not fit. Your job is to shrink it back to the size of the actual deal, the same way you would with a revision round that quietly became unlimited.

The price

Exclusivity is a priced item, priced by what it blocks

A fair clause costs extra because it costs you something real: the other bookings in that category, in that place, during that window. That is the pricing logic, and it is the same logic behind the rate you set for a post. The post is priced by the work of making it. Exclusivity is priced by the work it prevents.

Illustrative example, labeled as such: you charge $150 for a post. A clause asks you not to post for any other coffee shop in your market for 30 days. If two similar shops would likely have booked you in that month, the clause is not a footnote to a $150 job. It is closer to a $300 block on top of it. You can accept that trade at a price that reflects it, shrink the window to a week so the block gets smaller, or decline the clause and sell the post alone. All three are professional answers. Giving the block away free inside the $150 is the only bad one.

Notice the trade you can offer, which is the same move as in negotiating without an agent: do not argue about the word exclusivity. Trade its size. A narrower category, a smaller place, or a shorter window is worth less, so it can cost less. The brand picks the size it actually needs, and pays for that size, not for the biggest version a template could imagine.

The rewrite

One sentence turns a bad clause into a fair one

You do not need to redline a contract to fix this. You need one written sentence that names the category, the place, the end date, and the price, sent in the same thread where the rest of the deal lives. It can sound like this:

"Happy to add exclusivity in this shape: no posts for another coffee shop in this market from the posting date through 14 days after it, for $75 on top of the post rate. Outside that category, that market, and those dates, I am free to take other work. Reply yes here and I will add it to our agreed terms."

Read what that sentence does. The business still gets the thing it actually wanted: a clear run where you are its only voice in that narrow spot. You keep every other category, every other place, and every date after the window. And because the clause is a line item, it can be renewed, extended, or dropped next time without reopening the whole deal. That is what fair looks like in practice: specific, short, and paid.

Exclusivity FAQ

What else do creators ask about exclusivity?

What is a creator exclusivity clause?

A creator exclusivity clause is a promise that you will not work with certain other brands for a set period. In a fair version, the clause names exactly who or what is off limits, where that limit applies, and when it ends. If any of those three is missing, the clause is not a small extra. It is an open ended block on your future work, and it should be rewritten before you sign or agree in writing.

What makes an exclusivity clause fair for a local creator?

Three limits and a price. The category is narrow, for example one type of business rather than a whole industry. The place is clear, for example the same market rather than everywhere you post. The window is short and has a real end date, tied to the campaign rather than open ended. And exclusivity is paid as its own line item, because it blocks other bookings you could have taken during that window.

How long should creator exclusivity last?

Only as long as the reason for it lasts. For a local post, that usually means a short window around the posting date, long enough for the business to get the benefit of being your only pick in that narrow category and place, and no longer. A window with no end date, or one that runs for many months after a single post, asks you to give up a season of work for one booking. Shorten the window, narrow the category, or raise the price until the trade is honest.

Should a creator charge extra for exclusivity?

Yes. Exclusivity is not a favor tucked into a standard rate. It is a separate, priced item, because every day it runs can cost you another booking in the same category. Price it by what it blocks: a narrow category, a small place, and a few days is a modest add on. A broad category, a wide area, or a long window should cost much more, and some versions are not worth taking at any price a local business can fairly pay.
