For businesses · Music & Labels

A release drops everywhere at once. The people talking about it should live where it lands.

Record label marketing usually buys one big national audience that lives somewhere else. Magpie works the other way: you pick the cities, and local creators ranked by local reach in each city form your music street team for local music promotion, posting the release or the show to followers who actually live there.

A national follower count does not fill a room or break a song in a new city. Magpie ranks creators by local reach in each market, the share of their followers who live there, so the person posting about your single, your album or your show date is talking to people who can actually come out.

How it works

From the release calendar to the feed

/ 01

Pick the cities and the moment

You name the cities, the release date or the show dates, and the songs and venues behind them. Whether you want to promote a new release, build show promotion around a date, or both, the moment goes on the booking first, so every post is timed to land around it.

/ 02

The street team is ranked by local reach

In each city, the street team is the creators whose followers actually live there, ranked by local reach ahead of raw follower count. The brief, the links and any guest list or merch comp are set on one card both sides confirm before anyone posts.

/ 03

Posts land around the moment

A tracked booking timed to the release or the show: 1 post or reel plus 1 story that shares the post, honestly told and fully disclosed.* If a week passes with no post, the booking is replaced and does not count against your plan.

*Creators have up to one week to post. The goal is to post within 48 hours of the moment the booking is timed to, and hitting it earns their Momentum. If a week passes with no post, the booking is replaced and does not count against your plan.

Why local for music

Music breaks city by city

/ 01

Shows sell locally

A room is filled by people who live near it. The crowd for a show comes from the city and the drive around it, so the promotion that fills it has to reach people there, not an audience spread across the country.

/ 02

Scenes are local

Scenes break artists city by city: the venues, the openers, the college stations and the people who go out every week. Local creators are inside that scene, and their post about a show reads like a tip from a friend, because to their followers it is one.

/ 03

Local followers actually come out

A follower three states away cannot buy a ticket for a show on Tuesday. A follower across town can. Local reach is the share of a creator's audience that can hear about the show and actually be in the room, and that is the number Magpie ranks by.

Booked dates

Sell out the room

A date on the calendar is only half the job. The other half is a room full of people who live close enough to come out on a weeknight. When an act has a gig booked in a city where its following is thin, the street team in that city carries the show to the people who actually go out there.

/ 01

The announcement lands locally

The date goes on the booking card with the venue and the ticket link, and local creators post the announcement to followers who live in that city. The show enters the local conversation through people the city already follows, ranked by local reach there, not through a post aimed at a national audience somewhere else.

/ 02

The countdown builds

In the weeks before the date, creators keep the show in front of the city: the songs on their stories, the countdown to the night, the friends they are bringing. It reads as locals getting excited about a night out, because that is what it is.

/ 03

Week of, and day of

The push tightens as the date lands: week-of posts, day-of stories, and stories from the room itself once the show is on. The next city on the route sees a crowd that showed up, posted by people who were standing in it.

/ 04

Counted, never promised

Creators are comped with tickets or a guest list spot, or booked paid at their own listed rate. Magpie counts the posts and verifies the check-ins where a venue visit is part of the booking. What Magpie does not do is promise ticket sales. The posts are counted; the room is yours to read.

Example: a booked date, three weeks out

Example: an out-of-town act has a club date in one city three weeks from now, and almost no following there yet. The date, the venue and the ticket link go on one booking card. Local creators in that city announce the show to followers who live there, count it down in the weeks before, and post stories from the room on the night. The act's team watches every post land in one dashboard, counted as it happens, and reads the night against its own ticket count.

New cities

Get booked in a new city

Promoters book what they can already picture working in their room. An act with no history in a city has no local story to point to, so the first job is building one: visible local demand that a venue or promoter can see before they take the chance. It is not a guarantee of a booking. It is the local proof promoters look for, built in the open.

/ 01

Start with the music, locally

Local creators post the songs to followers who live in the city: the track on a story, the song in a post about their own week, the listening moment shared the way they share anything they are into. The music starts living in the city before the act ever plays there.

/ 02

Demand a promoter can see

Posts from people in the city, talking to people in the city, add up to something a promoter can actually look at: local interest, in public, from accounts whose audiences are verifiably local. That is the proof of demand bookers ask for when an unknown act comes calling.

/ 03

A first date with a head start

When the first date does get booked, it does not start cold. The same creators who built the buzz become the street team for the show, and the room has already heard of the act from people they follow.

Honest FAQ

Questions, answered straight

How is this different from a traditional street team agency or a national influencer platform?

A traditional street team agency works one city and one release at a time, with a roster it built itself and reporting you have to take on trust. A national influencer platform ranks creators by follower count, wherever those followers happen to live. Magpie ranks creators by local reach in each city, so your street team in every city is made of people whose followers actually live there, and you run the same release or show play across many cities from one platform, with every post tracked the same way.

What do creators actually post, and do they have to like the music?

Each booking is one post or reel plus one story that shares the post, in the creator's own words, sharing the track, the release or the show date with their local followers. Creators choose the bookings they want, and no creator on Magpie is ever required to post something positive or to say they love a song. Posts are honest and personal, and every post carries full disclosure that the visit, the product or the post was comped or paid. That honesty is exactly what makes the posts believable to the people reading them.

What does the comp look like for music?

It is your call, set on the booking card before any creator claims it. Comp can be a guest list spot, show tickets, merch or vinyl, the same way a meal or a service is the comp for a local business. Or you can skip comp and book a creator paid at their own listed rate. Either way the terms sit on one card both sides confirm first: what posts, by when, what is included, and when money moves.

What usage rights do we get?

On a standard booking you can share the delivered post natively, with the creator credited. Paid bookings carry the standard license: repost rights plus ad use of the creator's content for 90 days. Never perpetuity. If you need the content for longer, you renew the license with the creator rather than owning the post forever.

How do we know it worked?

You see what Magpie can count, counted: posts live, tracked city by city in your dashboard, and check-ins where a venue visit is part of the booking. Magpie does not claim streams, ticket sales or chart impact it cannot see. You read the tracked posts alongside your own streaming and ticket numbers, which stay yours.

Can independent artists use this, not just labels?

Yes. Independent artists and their teams come through the same door as labels, on the same published pricing, with the same street team ranked by local reach in each city. If you run your own promo, you pick the cities and the moment yourself, and the brief, the comp and the terms still sit on one card.

How fast can a street team spin up before a show date?

As fast as a booking can be set up. The date, the venue, the ticket link and the comp go on one card, creators in that city claim it, and the posts are timed to the date: the goal is posts landing within 48 hours of the moment a booking is timed to, with one week as the outside limit. Three weeks out leaves comfortable room for an announcement, a countdown and a week-of push. Closer in, the same posts still land, just tighter together. One honest note: markets activate as their founding rosters complete, so the cities available are the ones whose rosters have formed.

What does the act need to provide?

The date and the venue, the ticket link, and the music itself: the tracks or clips creators can share, plus any artwork or photos you want in the posts. Then the comp, set on the booking card before anyone claims it: tickets, guest list spots, merch or vinyl, or a paid booking at the creator's own listed rate. Everything sits on one card both sides confirm, so a creator never posts against a brief that lives in someone's inbox.

What do you measure for a show?

Posts live, tracked in your dashboard, and check-ins counted where a venue visit is part of the booking. That is the whole list. Magpie does not measure ticket sales, and it does not claim them: your ticket count is yours, and you read it alongside the counted posts. What Magpie gives you is proof the city heard about the show, from people who live there, on the record.
